Michael Monaco — Interview Questions and Answers
=================================================

TELL ME ABOUT YOURSELF
----------------------
Mike Monaco is a senior cloud consultant and architect with over 25 years of
software engineering experience. He is currently Manager of Cloud Consultants
at Instem, a pharmaceutical life sciences company, where he leads a team of 5
consultants delivering AWS and Azure cloud solutions for pharma clients.

He holds a BS in Computer Science from NC State University, a JD from North
Carolina Central University, and an AWS Certified Solutions Architect Associate
certification earned in 2024.

His career spans 18 years building products at SAS Institute — including
directing the development of SAS Studio, the browser-based IDE now used by
hundreds of thousands of SAS users globally — followed by several years in
pharmaceutical cloud consulting at d-wise and Instem. He also runs Mike's Cloud
Solutions, a YouTube channel with 120,000 subscribers and 3.5 million views
covering AWS, Azure, GCP, and OCI.

GREATEST TECHNICAL ACCOMPLISHMENT — SAS STUDIO
-----------------------------------------------
In 2012, Mike was asked to build a replacement for SAS Learning Edition — a
lightweight interface for academic users. What started as SAS Studio for
Academics became something far larger.

The team conceived the product at SAS Global Forum and delivered the first
working version in six months. It was well-received enough that SAS leadership
decided to expand scope. Two years later, SAS Studio was no longer an academic
tool — it was the default interface for SAS, used by hundreds of thousands of
users globally and shipped as part of the core SAS platform.

Mike managed a team of 15 engineers through that entire arc — but stayed deeply
embedded in the architecture, the framework design, and the technical decisions.
This is the clearest example of his "colonel, not a general" philosophy: he was
accountable for the team's delivery while remaining hands-on at the code and
architecture level. Going from "academic replacement product" to "default
interface for the world's largest privately held software company" in under
three years is the accomplishment he is most proud of.

HANDLING DIFFICULT CLIENT SITUATIONS — SCOPE CREEP
---------------------------------------------------
The most common difficult client situation in consulting is not a technical
failure — it is scope creep. The pattern is almost always the same: the client
asks for one small additional thing, you want to be a team player, you say yes.
Then another small thing. Then another. A dozen small agreements later and the
project scope has changed significantly, but nobody has explicitly acknowledged
it.

Mike's approach is to manage this proactively rather than reactively. When a
client asks for something outside the original agreement, he calls it out at
the moment it is asked — not weeks later when the schedule has already slipped.
He will say something like: "That is not in the current SOW — we can do it, but
let us talk about what that means for timeline and budget before we commit."

He also does periodic SOW reviews during active engagements — going back to the
original statement of work and comparing it against what is actually being
delivered. This surfaces drift before it becomes a crisis.

The balance he strikes is being a genuine team player — clients should feel he
is on their side — while being honest about what is realistic. Saying yes to
everything feels collaborative in the moment but leads to a failed engagement.
A good consultant protects the client from that outcome, even when the client
is the one asking for more.

HOW MIKE APPROACHES A NEW CLOUD MIGRATION ENGAGEMENT
-----------------------------------------------------
Every engagement starts with a discovery period. The goal is to deeply
understand the client's domain, existing technology stack, and what success
actually looks like for their stakeholders. This involves structured
conversations with technical and business stakeholders, and sometimes
researching technology specific to the client's environment.

During discovery, Mike favors building MVPs — minimum viable products that give
stakeholders hands-on experience with a reasonable representation of the final
solution. This surfaces misaligned expectations early, when they are cheap to
fix, rather than late when they are expensive.

Discovery ends with a formal architecture document and validated requirements.
In regulated pharmaceutical environments, this also includes settling on a
validation strategy — drafting IQ (Installation Qualification), OQ (Operational
Qualification), and PQ (Performance Qualification) documents before any
production work begins.

Execution then follows a structured sequence: validation environment first,
then production, then DR. Each phase is deliberate.

At the end of the engagement, Mike prioritizes a formal support handoff before
go-live. Consultants remain available during a hypercare period — a window where
the delivery team is directly involved in support alongside the client's
operations team. The length of hypercare depends on how well the client's
support team is trained, which is directly tied to documentation quality.
Better documentation means a shorter hypercare window and a cleaner exit.

LEADERSHIP STYLE — COLONEL NOT A GENERAL
-----------------------------------------
Mike describes his leadership style as "a colonel, not a general." He leads by
doing — staying technically hands-on and contributing meaningfully at the code
and architecture level regardless of whether he is in an individual contributor
or team lead role. He has managed teams of up to 15 engineers but always
maintains deep technical involvement. He is not interested in pure people
management divorced from technical work.

DISAGREEING WITH TECHNICAL DIRECTION — CONSULTING VS PRODUCT MINDSET
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Mike has worked on both sides of this divide — 18 years building products at
SAS Institute, followed by several years in pure consulting at d-wise and
Instem. That dual background gives him a clear view of where companies go wrong
when they try to do both simultaneously.

In consulting, a degree of confidence ahead of certainty is normal and healthy.
A consultant commits to delivering something that exists in the technology
landscape and figures out the specific implementation during the engagement.
That is not dishonesty — the technology is real, the consultant is capable, and
delivery follows.

The problem comes when that same mindset gets applied to product development.
Shipping a product requires fundamentally different discipline — roadmaps,
repeatability, QA, versioning, customer support at scale. "We will figure it
out" works for a one-time consulting engagement. It does not work when you are
promising a feature to a hundred customers on a release date.

Mike has seen companies attempt this transition without changing how they think
about commitments and delivery. The instinct that made them great consultants
becomes a liability in product development. The symptoms look like execution
problems — missed dates, quality issues — but the root cause is cultural.
Having built real products at SAS and then spent years in consulting, he can
recognize that mismatch quickly.

HOW MIKE STAYS CURRENT IN CLOUD TECHNOLOGY
-------------------------------------------
Mike's primary mechanism for staying current is his YouTube channel. The
process is deliberate: he identifies a technology or architecture pattern he
wants to understand deeply, builds a production-quality working demonstration,
publishes the code to GitHub, and then produces a video walkthrough.

This is not passive learning — building something real and explaining it
publicly forces a level of understanding that reading documentation never does.
If it does not work, it does not ship.

The side effect of this approach is a growing library of what Mike calls "lego
blocks" — reference implementations across AWS, Azure, GCP, and OCI covering
serverless, containers, identity, AI, networking, desktop environments, and
more. These are not toy examples. They are the same patterns he reaches for
when a client engagement calls for them.

With 120,000 subscribers and 3.5 million views, the channel has also become a
useful signal that the content is genuinely valuable to other practitioners.
You can explore the full lego block toolbox by asking this assistant about
specific technologies or architectures.
